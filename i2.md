Just two wires — that's all it takes to talk to dozens of sensors, displays, and peripherals at once. I2C is one of those fundamentals that shows up in almost every robot project worth building.

## What is I2C?

I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit, often spoken as "I-squared-C") is a serial communication protocol invented by Philips in 1982. It uses just two lines: **SDA** (data) and **SCL** (clock). Every device on the bus gets a unique 7-bit address, so a single pair of wires can serve up to 112 devices simultaneously.

Speeds are modest — 100 kHz in standard mode, 400 kHz in fast mode — but that's plenty for sensors, OLED displays, real-time clocks, and motor controllers. Most microcontrollers used in robotics (Raspberry Pi Pico, ESP32, Arduino) have dedicated I2C hardware built in, so it costs you nothing extra in silicon.

A key feature: I2C is a **bus**. Chain a temperature sensor, a compass, an OLED, and a distance sensor all on the same two pins. Each responds only to its own address. Short projects rarely need more than a handful, but it's reassuring to know the headroom is there.

## Why robot builders care

Robots need to sense their environment, and sensors speak I2C. The BME280 (temperature, humidity, pressure), the MPU-6050 IMU, SSD1306 OLED displays, VL53L0X time-of-flight sensors — they all use I2C. Without it, you'd run out of GPIO pins before you ran out of ideas.

There's a practical bonus, too: **bus scanning**. Before writing a single line of driver code you can scan the bus and get back a list of addresses — instant confirmation that your hardware is wired correctly. That scan loop has saved more debugging sessions than any oscilloscope.

Pull-up resistors matter here. SDA and SCL need to be pulled high (typically 4.7 kΩ) for the bus to work. Many breakout boards include them, but if you're building raw circuits you'll need to add them yourself.

## Get started

The fastest path to your first I2C project is an OLED display and a Pico. Wire SDA to GP4, SCL to GP5, load MicroPython, and within five minutes you can be printing text to a tiny screen. That same wiring approach scales directly to any I2C sensor you pick up later.

Kevin's [Talking to the World — Working with I2C and SPI](/learn/i2c_spi/00_intro.html) course walks you through everything from bus scanning and reading datasheets to building a real sensor dashboard with a BME280. It's the most thorough treatment on the site and a great companion whether you're just starting or want to fill in gaps.

For a fun visual target, the [Adding OLED Displays with MicroPython](/blog/micropython-oled-displays.html) post shows how to drive an SSD1306 over I2C — text, graphics, and live sensor readouts all on a display roughly the size of a postage stamp.

Once I2C clicks, SPI (its faster, four-wire sibling) will feel familiar. And when you start chaining sensors into a real robot brain, you'll be glad this particular reaction only needs two wires.
