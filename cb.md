There's no rulebook that says robots have to be polite. Combat robotics is where engineering meets full-contact sport — and it's one of the most exhilarating ways to put your maker skills to the test.

## What is Combat Robotics?

Combat robotics means building a robot specifically to fight other robots inside an arena. Matches are won by disabling, flipping, or immobilising the opponent before time runs out.

Robots are organised by **weight class**. The most accessible classes for makers are:

- **Antweight** — 150 g (UK) or 1 lb (US). Fits in your hand. Cheap to build and repair.
- **Beetleweight** — 1.36 kg (3 lb). Small enough to post, serious enough to cause real damage.
- **Featherweight** — 13.6 kg (30 lb). Proper competition weight, closer to what you'd see on TV.

Most first-time builders start at antweight or beetleweight — the parts are affordable, and you can print or laser-cut the chassis at home.

Weapons break down into a few flavours: **spinners** use a heavy rotating disc or bar to store kinetic energy (and release it catastrophically on contact), **flippers** use a pneumatic or electric arm to toss the opponent into the air, and **wedges** simply push the other robot around. Armour material matters enormously — HDPE, polycarbonate, and titanium each have different weight-to-toughness trade-offs.

## Why robot builders care

Combat robotics rewards *every* skill in your toolkit at once. You'll need to:

- **Design a chassis** that's both light and tough — CAD and 3D printing are essential here.
- **Pick and wire motors** that deliver the torque you need without blowing your weight budget.
- **Drive with precision** using an H-bridge or ESC and a radio receiver.
- **Survive the arena** — which means thinking about armour, self-righting mechanisms, and fail-safes.

It's also a fantastic forcing function. Nothing teaches you about structural integrity faster than watching your chassis shatter in the arena and having to redesign it overnight.

## Get started

The most practical entry point is to **build a printed antweight chassis**. At 150 g you can experiment freely — a full rebuild after an arena session costs very little. Start with a simple wedge: no weapon to worry about, just a low wedge-shaped chassis that slides under opponents. Master the drivetrain first.

The **[SMARS course](/learn/smars/00_intro.html)** is a great foundation — it covers differential drive, motor wiring, and chassis design in a printable format that translates directly to combat chassis thinking.

For the electronics, the [H-Bridge element](/periodic-table/hb.html) explains the motor driver circuit you'll need, and the [DC Motors element](/periodic-table/dc.html) covers how to choose motors with the right speed-to-torque balance for your weight class.

To design your own chassis from scratch, the **[Build a SMARS Robot in Fusion 360](/learn/smars_fusion360/00_intro.html)** course teaches the CAD workflow.

Look up your nearest combat robotics club — the community is welcoming to first-time builders. Bring your bot, expect it to get destroyed, learn from every hit, and come back with a better version. That cycle *is* the sport.
