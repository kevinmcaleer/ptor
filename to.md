You can build a remarkable amount with surprisingly few tools. The trick is knowing which ones actually matter — and getting comfortable with them before your first big project.

## What are the essential maker tools?

"Tools" covers everything from a humble set of screwdrivers to precision test equipment. For robot builders, the core kit breaks down into three groups:

**Hand tools** — screwdrivers (Phillips and flat-head in several sizes), pliers (needle-nose and standard), wire cutters and wire strippers, and a good set of hex keys (M2, M2.5, and M3 cover most robot hardware). A digital calliper is worth every penny too.

**Measuring and testing** — a digital multimeter is the single most important tool in any electronics workshop. Use it to check voltage, measure resistance, and confirm continuity before you plug anything in. A bench power supply with adjustable voltage and a current-limiting setting saves components (and sometimes fingers) when you're testing a new circuit.

**Assembly and rework** — a soldering iron with a fine tip (350–380 °C is a good starting point), flux, and quality solder (60/40 tin-lead or lead-free 63/37). A desoldering pump or wick sorts out mistakes without drama.

## Why robot builders care

Robots are physical things. No matter how good your code is, if a motor wire isn't properly crimped or a standoff is cross-threaded, the build will let you down. Good tools make clean work — and clean work means fewer debugging sessions chasing a loose connection.

A multimeter alone can answer 80% of the questions that beginners spend hours Googling: "Is this pin actually outputting 3.3 V?", "Is this motor drawing too much current?", "Why isn't my LED lighting up?" The answer is almost always a measurement away.

Learning to solder properly unlocks the whole world of pre-made modules — motor drivers, sensor breakouts, display boards — that snap your robot together in an afternoon instead of a weekend.

## Get started

Start small. Buy a decent digital multimeter (brands like Uni-T or Fluke are both reliable), a soldering iron kit (the TS80 or Pinecil are popular with UK makers), and a set of precision screwdrivers. That covers the first six months of most builds.

The [10 Tips for Getting Started with Robotics](/blog/10-tips-for-getting-started-with-robotics.html) post is a great read alongside this — it covers tools in the context of a full beginner's journey, including why a multimeter and bench power supply are listed as essentials.

When you're organising your workspace as it grows, [The Self-Documenting Workshop](/blog/self-documenting-workshop.html) has some clever ideas for keeping track of what you have, where it lives, and how it all connects together.

If you need a quick reference on component sizes — because knowing whether that screw is M2.5 or M3 before you strip the head is genuinely useful — [Common Part Dimensions](/blog/common-part-dimensions.html) is a handy bookmark.

Build the habit early: before you power anything up, reach for the multimeter first. Five seconds of checking saves five hours of fault-finding.
